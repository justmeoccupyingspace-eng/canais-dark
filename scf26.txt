Executive Summary

The Federal Reserve Board’s triennial Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) collects information
about U.S. family income, net worth, balance sheet components, credit use, and other financial
outcomes.1 The Federal Reserve has sponsored the modern version of this survey of U.S. families
every three years since 1989.2


As context for the survey results, over the three years between the 2022 and 2025 surveys, real
gross domestic product grew at an annual rate of around 2½ percent, the civilian unemployment
rate rose from 3.7 percent to 4.2 percent, and the annual rate of change in the consumer price
index averaged 3.6 percent.3 Against this backdrop of moderate economic expansion and elevated
inflation, the 2025 SCF indicates that while most families experienced moderate increases in
income and net worth, an increasing subset of families appear to have been experiencing finan-
cial stress.


Key findings in this report include the following:


Income
• Real median family income, which is measured for the year before the survey, rose 7 percent
    between the 2022 to 2025 surveys, while real mean family income fell 6 percent.4
• Families in the lower ends of the income and net worth distributions saw modest increases in
    median and mean income, while families in the upper ends saw declines. These patterns indi-
    cate that income inequality decreased slightly between surveys.
• Median income generally exhibited broad-based gains across families with different characteris-
    tics, while changes in mean income showed less of a consistent pattern. For example, while all
    age groups saw growth in median income, with particularly strong growth among the oldest
    families, mean income remained about unchanged for some age groups while sharply declining
    for others. The main exception to the general growth in median income is for families grouped
    by race or ethnicity, where Black non-Hispanic families and Asian families experienced declines
    in median and mean income.

    Net Worth
    • Between 2022 and 2025, real median net worth (wealth) rose by 2 percent, while real mean
      net worth rose more, by 7 percent. On balance, wealth inequality appears little changed
      since 2022.
    • In contrast to income, changes in wealth largely reinforced existing patterns of wealth inequality
      by family characteristics. Families that typically have less wealth (such as young families,
      renters, and families without a high school diploma) saw median and, oftentimes, mean wealth
      declines. Meanwhile, compared with families in other groups, families that typically have more
      wealth (such as families at the top of the income distribution, older families, and those at the
      upper end of the wealth distribution) saw some of the largest median and mean wealth gains.


    Assets
    • The homeownership rate was 66 percent in 2025, about unchanged from 2022. For families
      that owned a home, the median net housing value (the value of a home minus home-secured
      debt) rose to $230,000 in 2025 from $218,900 in 2022. Housing affordability remained near
      its historical low, as the median home was worth more than 4.5 times the median
      family income.
    • Retirement plan participation, which includes account-type plans, individual retirement
      accounts, or defined benefit plans, was up slightly between 2022 and 2025 at around 65 per-
      cent. For families with account-type plans, mean and median balances rose. For younger fami-
      lies, retirement plan participation across plan types continued to increase.
    • Participation in the stock market declined slightly from 58 percent in 2022 to 56 percent in
      2025. The decrease in participation was driven by families outside the top 10 percent of the
      income distribution. Conditional on holding stock, median stock holdings grew 36 percent from
      $56,900 in 2022 to $77,400 in 2025.
    • In 2025, more than 15 percent of all families owned a privately held business, a slight increase
      from 2022. This increase in privately held businesses was driven by families in the bottom
      90 percent of income. That said, only 8 percent of families in the bottom half of the income dis-
      tribution and 40 percent of families in the top decile of the income distribution owned a pri-
      vately held business. Conditional on owning a business, the mean value was lower in 2025
      than in 2022 for families in the bottom 90 percent of income and was about unchanged for the
      top 10 percent.

Debt
• Despite an environment of rising interest rates on loans to households, the fraction of families
  with any type of debt remained about stable at 77 percent. Median and mean debt outstanding
  remained unchanged.
• Total debt secured by a primary residence increased between 2022 and 2025. While about
  42 percent of families in both 2022 and 2025 had debt secured by their primary residence,
  among these families the median amount of this debt increased from $170,200 to $183,600,
  and the mean increased from $232,500 to $239,700.
• Total credit card debt also increased. While the share of families with credit card debt—families
  with a balance on their credit cards after their last payment—was about unchanged at 45 per-
  cent, median credit card debt increased $150 to $3,100 and mean credit card debt rose
  $1,300 to $8,000.
• The share of families that had student debt in 2025 was 20 percent, down 2 percentage points
  from 2022. Among families with student debt, median and mean balances also declined 5 and
  16 percent, to $25,600 and $43,000, respectively.


Financial Vulnerability
• Borrowers look less financially fragile when their debts are scaled to their assets rather than to
  their income. For debtors, the median leverage ratio—a family’s total debt relative to its total
  assets—was 26.9 percent, continuing the steady decline seen since the 2013 survey. However,
  the median debt payment-to-income ratio was 15.4 percent, up 2.0 percentage points from
  2022, which is an increase that may be related to the concurrent increase in interest rates for
  mortgages and consumer loans. The total debt-to-income ratio also increased from 89.4 per-
  cent in 2022 to 94.9 percent in 2025.
• The fraction of families with debt payment-to-income ratios greater than 40 percent, which are
  families with particularly high debt payment obligations relative to their incomes, increased from
  6.5 to 8.6 percent, a level last seen in the 2013 survey.
• Families’ propensity to stay current on their financial obligations worsened between 2022 and
  2025. Families were more likely to be behind on their financial obligations than at any point
  since the 2010 survey. Between the 2022 and 2025 surveys, the share of families that
  reported being behind on loan payments sharply increased from about 12 percent to almost
  20 percent. Furthermore, more than 8 percent reported being two months late or more, up from
  5 percent in 2022.

The SCF is designed to capture families across the net worth (wealth) distribution, including fami-
lies that have extremely high wealth. As a consequence, the SCF also captures the small set of
families with extremely high income, since families with high wealth are likely to have high income.


Generally, when data include very large values for a relatively small part of the population—as is
the case for income, net worth, and many of the other items considered in this report—estimates
of the median are often statistically less sensitive to such very large values than are estimates of
the mean. At the same time, means are generally more useful for comparing across population
subgroups, because every member of the group contributes equally to the overall average. In the
case of income, changes in mean income are more affected than changes in median income by
shifts in the income of families with extremely high income. Indeed, between the 2022 and 2025
surveys, the fall in mean income was driven by a decline for families at the top of the income dis-
tribution. In fact, excluding families in the top 1 percent of income indicates that, between the
2022 and 2025 surveys, mean income declined only 1 percent.5

    The SCF income measure reflects a family’s before-tax and inflation-adjusted income for the full
    calendar year preceding the survey year. This measure aggregates reported income from specific
    sources, most of which correspond to items on an income tax form.6 The SCF also includes ques-
    tions to measure a family’s “usual income” because, in any given year, a family’s income may
    reflect a recent spell of unemployment, a bonus from an employer, a capital loss or gain on invest-
    ments, or other transitory factors.7 In the 2022 survey, 28 percent of families reported that their
    income was unusual, a jump of 5 percentage points that was likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic
    economy and the associated pandemic-related policies that were put into place. In the 2025
    survey, 23 percent of families reported that their income was unusual, which was a return to the
    pre-pandemic share.8


    The SCF also includes a question that asks whether families have a good idea of their income for
    the next year, as a way to measure families’ uncertainty over their future income. The share of
    families that did not have a good idea of their income for the next year has continued to increase
    since the 2019 survey, to a new SCF high of 37 percent in 2025.9

Box 1. Family Expenditures across Income and Wealth
The 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) added new questions on total nonhousing expenditures,
as well as a new question on vacation spending. Along with existing questions on food, housing, and
automobile expenditures, these questions provide more comprehensive measures of family spending.1
With respect to total nonhousing expenditure (hereafter “total spending,” for short), families spent
$48,700, on average, over the previous 12 months. Median total spending was $33,700 over the same
period.2

Average total spending was substantially higher for families with higher usual income. Spending among
families in the bottom quintile of the usual income distribution was $17,600, 7.2 percent of overall
spending. Total spending increased steadily up the income distribution to $68,700 among families in
the 80th to 90th percentiles of usual income (figure A). Total expenditures were highly concentrated
among households in the top decile of usual income, who spent an average of $138,800, nearly
double the spending of the 80th to 90th percentiles, and accounted for 28.5 percent of overall
spending.

Net Worth by Family Characteristics
Higher-income families tend to have higher net worth, as these families generally save more,
which can result in a feedback effect of higher income from the accumulated assets. Furthermore,
net worth tends to increase and then plateau (or decline) over the life cycle, reflecting changes in
savings behavior that occur with age as individuals save for retirement throughout their working
career and then spend those savings in retirement. Finally, net worth differs by family characteris-
tics that include educational attainment, race or ethnic background, housing status, and urba-
nicity. These cross-sectional differences in net worth across families generally mirror patterns
seen in income, but the gaps are larger. For example, families in which the reference person had a
college degree had 1.9 times the median income of those with some college but almost 3 times
the median net worth.

Between the 2022 and 2025 surveys, grouping families by usual income revealed that lower-
income families saw some declines in their wealth, while higher-income families experienced
gains. For families with usual income in the lowest quintile, median and mean net worth fell 6 and
4 percent, respectively. In contrast, families closer to the middle of the usual income distribution,
between the 40th and 80th percentiles of usual income, saw median and mean net worth grow
5 to 15 percent. For families in the top decile of the usual income distribution, family median net
worth grew 31 percent to $3,669,900, while mean net worth rose less strongly, by 6 percent. The
pattern of changes in net worth across the usual income distribution stood in contrast to 2019 to
2022, where net worth saw broad-based growth across the distribution.


Net worth declined substantially for the youngest families, while exhibiting large gains for the
oldest. Families younger than 35 saw median net worth decrease 23 percent to $33,000 and
mean net worth fall 33 percent to $135,400. These declines in net worth among young families
are primarily driven by the unwinding of business equity gains between 2019 and 2022. In con-
trast, families older than 75 saw substantial gains in both median and mean net worth (37 per-
cent and 10 percent, respectively). The rise in net worth for the oldest age group is so large rela-
tive to younger age groups that, in contrast with the previous few survey years, families aged 75
or older had the largest net worth, exceeding levels by families that are nearly or recently retired
(which saw some declines).18 For families in the remaining age groups, changes in median net
worth ranged from some declines to modest gains, while mean net worth mostly grew.


Grouping families by educational attainment, median net worth generally changed more, in percent
terms, than mean net worth across education groups. Families with no high school diploma experi-
enced the largest percent changes in median and mean net worth, with median net worth falling
55 percent while mean net worth rose 53 percent. The fall in median net worth for these families
was their largest percent decline recorded in the modern SCF, undoing the gain these families saw
between 2019 and 2022. Families with a high school diploma saw the same pattern of changes
of median and mean net worth as those with no high school diploma, though the size of the
changes were less extreme. The declines in median net worth and increases in mean net worth
imply that wealth inequality within these groups increased. In contrast, families with some college
education but no degree saw the largest gains in median and mean net worth, while families with
a college degree had more stable median and mean net worth. On net, the substantial gap in
mean net worth between families with and without a college degree remained about constant
between surveys.

Box 2. Retirement Plan Participation
Retirement plan participation can increase families’ net worth and, thus, provide future financial secu-
rity through lower taxes on savings, a guaranteed income in retirement, increased compensation from
employer contributions, or all the above. A family is considered as participating in a retirement plan if it
has any of the following: an individual retirement account (IRA); an account-type, defined contribution
(DC) pension through an employer, which includes both 401(k)s and 403(b)s; or a defined benefit (DB)
pension through a current or former employer. The following discussion only includes families with a ref-
erence person aged 64 or younger to capture families who generally have not retired.

Overall plan participation is 65.1 percent in 2025, up slightly from 64.4 percent in 2022. Plan partici-
pation is highest among older families, or those closest to retirement (figure A). In 2025, around
70 percent of families aged 45 to 64 and 66 percent of families aged 35 to 44 participated, similar or
slightly higher compared to rates in 2016. From 2010 through 2019, those closer to retirement age
saw falling retirement participation before rebounding in recent years, while families in the middle age
range (aged 35 to 44) saw modest gains. Meanwhile, younger families (those under age 35) have
notably increased their participation over the past 15 years.

Financial Vulnerability

The SCF data can be used to measure a family’s financial debt burden. In addition, the SCF col-
lects various measures of respondents’ recent experiences with credit markets, such as informa-
tion on credit applications and payment behavior.25 Understanding the extent of debt obligations
and the ways families interact with credit markets provides a window into a family’s financial vul-
nerabilities and offers indicators of potential financial distress.


Debt Burden
The ability of individual families to service their loans is a function of many factors, including the
level of their loan payments and the income and assets they have available to meet those pay-
ments. In planning their borrowing, families make assumptions about their future ability to repay
their loans. Problems may occur when events turn out to be contrary to those assumptions. If eco-
nomic shocks are sufficiently large and prevalent, a broad pattern of default, restraint in spending,
and financial distress in the wider economy might ensue.


The SCF data can be used to construct three measures of debt burdens: leverage ratios, debt-to-
income ratios, and payment-to-income ratios. Leverage ratios compare the amount of total debt to
total asset values, debt-to-income ratios compare the amount of debt relative to income, and
payment-to-income ratios compare payments made to service the debt relative to income. All three
ratios can be constructed either in aggregate or as a median among debtors.26


The aggregate leverage ratio ticked down, while the aggregate debt-to-income and payment-to-
income ratios increased between 2022 and 2025. In contrast, all three measures decreased
between 2019 to 2022 (table 5).27 In 2025, the median leverage ratio for debtors was 26.9 per-
cent, continuing the steady decline seen since the 2013 survey. The median debt-to-income ratio
for debtors, though, was 94.8, about unchanged since 2022, and the median debt payment-to-
income ratio for debtors was 15.4 percent, rising back up to its 2019 level.28

Credit Market Experiences
The SCF asks several questions that attempt to capture whether families are credit constrained,
two of which are (1) whether the family was turned down for credit over the past 12 months and
(2) whether the family decided not to apply for credit during the past 12 months for fear of being
turned down.29 In 2025, 10.1 percent of families responded “yes” to the first question,
unchanged from 2022. The share of families who responded “yes” to the second question rose
between 2022 and 2025, increasing slightly from 12.9 to 13.5 percent.


Families’ propensity to stay current on their financial obligations worsened sharply between 2022
and 2025, consistent with the increases in the aggregate debt-to-income and payment-to-income
ratios and further signaling increasing financial vulnerability among some families. Families were
more likely to be behind on such obligations than in any of the past four surveys, or since 2016.
Specifically, families that had any debt at the time of their interview are asked whether they were
behind on any of their loan payments in the preceding year. In 2025, 19.6 percent of families
reported being late on payments, a sharp increase from 12.2 percent in 2022. The share of fami-
lies that reported being 60 days late or more was 8.2 percent, more than a 3 percentage point
increase since 2022.


The SCF asks respondents whether anyone in the family took out a payday loan in the past year.
Payday loans are unsecured loans that are typically small and short term, and they generally carry
interest rates far exceeding those for conventional forms of credit. In 2025, 2.6 percent
of families reported taking out a payday loan, slightly higher than the 2.1 percent of families
in 2022.


Additional measures of financial distress that the SCF collects are whether families have declared
bankruptcy or experienced a foreclosure. In 2025, 1.4 percent of families reported having
declared bankruptcy in the past five years, which is largely unchanged from 2022. Seven-tenths of
a percent of families reported having foreclosure proceedings brought against properties they
owned in the past five years, a slight increase from 0.5 percent in 2022.30

Appendix A: The Data in This Report and
Statistical Measures

This report draws principally on the final data from the 2025 and 2022 Survey of Consumer
Finances (SCF). In the 2025 survey, 4,367 families were interviewed, and in the 2022 survey,
4,602 families were interviewed.31 To provide a larger context, some information is also included
from the final versions of earlier surveys.32 Differences between estimates from earlier
surveys—as reported here and as reported in previous Federal Reserve Bulletin articles and SCF
reports—are attributable to additional statistical processing, correction of minor data errors, revi-
sions to the survey weights, conceptual changes in the definitions of variables used in the
articles, and adjustments for inflation.


The survey collects information on families’ income before taxes for the calendar year preceding
the survey year. Otherwise, the bulk of these data cover the status of families as of the time of
the interview, including detailed information on their balance sheets and use of financial services
as well as on their pensions, labor force participation, and demographic characteristics. Most of
the core survey questionnaire has changed in only minor ways relevant to this report since 1989.
However, when the questionnaire has been modified at various points to enhance and update the
survey, every effort has been made to ensure the maximum degree of comparability of the data
over time. We provide information on key definitions and about how demographic and economic
groups are constructed for this report.


Definition of “Family”
The definition of “family” used throughout this report differs from that typically used in other gov-
ernment studies and is more comparable with the U.S. Census Bureau definition of
“households”—which can include one-person families—than with its use of “families.” In the SCF,
a household unit is divided into a primary economic unit (PEU)—the family—and everyone else in
the household. The PEU is intended to be the economically dominant single person or couple
(whether married or living together as partners) and all other persons in the household who are
financially interdependent with that economically dominant person or couple.

     Statistical Measures
     The principal detailed tables (tables 1 through 4) describing income, net worth, and asset and
     debt holdings focus on the percentage of various groups that have such items and the median and
     mean holding for those that have them.33


     One limitation of using the median as a descriptive device is that medians are not additive—that
     is, the sum of the medians of two items for the same population is generally not equal to the
     median of the sum (for example, median assets minus median liabilities will generally not equal
     median net worth). In contrast, means for a common population are additive. In the context of this
     report, where a comparable median and mean are given, the gain or loss of the mean relative to
     the median may usually be taken as indicative of the relative change at the top of the distribution;
     for example, when the mean exhibits larger growth than the median, it is typically taken to indicate
     that the values in the upper part of the distribution rose more than those in the lower part of the
     distribution.


     To provide a measure of the statistical significance of the developments discussed in this report,
     standard errors caused by sampling and imputation for missing data are given for selected esti-
     mates. Space limits prevent the inclusion of standard errors for all estimates. Although the statis-
     tical significance of the results generally is not addressed, the report highlights findings that are
     significant or are interesting in a broader context. Standard errors for all estimates in tables 1 and
     2 are available on the SCF website.

Measures of Debt Burden and Credit Market Experiences
The SCF includes several questions designed to capture information about respondents’ debt bur-
dens and interactions with credit markets. The specific concepts addressed in the SCF are neces-
sarily tied to the survey question wording and associated field interviewer instructions, which can
be found in the SCF codebook for the year(s) in question.42 What follows is a general exposition of
the debt burden and credit market experience measures reported in the tables.


Leverage ratios compare total debt with total assets. The aggregate version of this measure is the
sum of all debts for all families, divided by the sum of all assets for all families. The median for
debtors is the median of each individual family’s leverage ratio and is calculated for those with
positive values of total debt only, with families that have zero or negative assets assumed to have
an above-median leverage ratio.


The aggregate debt-to-income ratio is the sum of liabilities for all SCF families, divided by the total
income for all SCF families. The median for debtors is the 50th percentile among all individual
families’ debt-to-income ratios among families with positive values of total debt only, with families
that have zero or negative income assumed to have an above-median debt-to-income ratio.

Appendix B: Description of the Survey
and Survey Procedures

Data from the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) are the basis of the analysis presented in
this report. The SCF is a triennial interview survey of U.S. families sponsored by the Board of
Governors of the Federal Reserve System with the cooperation of the U.S. Department of the
Treasury.49 Since 1992, data for the SCF have been collected by NORC at the University of
Chicago (formerly, the National Opinion Research Center), a social science and survey research
organization. Although the majority of the data are collected between April and December of each
survey year, a small fraction of data are collected in the first part of the next calendar year. For the
2025 SCF, about one-seventh of the interviews were conducted between January and May 2026.
This fraction was smaller than the analogous fraction from the 2022 survey.


The SCF data used here are derived from the final internal version of the survey information. Data
from the 2025 survey, suitably altered to protect the privacy of respondents, along with additional
tabulations of data from the surveys beginning with 1989, are expected to be available in
October 2026 on the Federal Reserve Board’s website.50


Sampling
The survey is designed to provide a core set of data on family income, assets, and liabilities. The
need to measure financial characteristics imposes special requirements on the sample design for
the survey. The SCF is designed to provide reliable information on both attributes that are broadly
distributed in the population (such as homeownership) and attributes that are highly concentrated
in a relatively small part of the population (such as closely held businesses). The major aspects of
the sample design that address this requirement have been largely constant since 1989.


The SCF combines two techniques for random sampling. First, a standard multistage, area-
probability sample (a geographically based random sample) is selected to provide good coverage
of characteristics, such as homeownership, vehicles, and checking accounts, that are broadly dis-
tributed in the population.

The Interviews and Survey Questionnaire
Although questions have been modified and new questions added over time, the core of the
survey questionnaire, which covers family income, assets, and liabilities, has changed in only
minor ways since 1989. Changes to the questionnaire generally include instances in which the
structure was altered to accommodate changes in financial behaviors, changes in types of finan-
cial arrangements available to families, and changes in regulations covering data collection.


In 2016, interview sections on educational attainment, education loans, payment methods, and
financial institutions were revised, and additional questions addressing financial literacy among
respondents, parental educational attainment, and decisions under hypothetical financial situa-
tions were included.54 For all changes, every effort has been made to ensure the maximum degree
of comparability of the data over time. Except where noted in the report, the data are highly com-
parable over time.


The generosity of families in giving their time for interviews has been crucial to the ongoing suc-
cess of the SCF. In the 2025 SCF, the median interview length was about 120 minutes; the
increase over the 2022 SCF (110 minutes) was primarily due to the addition of the new questions
on families’ actual spending over the last year, families’ hypothetical rates of spending, saving,
and paying down debt due to unexpected income, and families’ informal support networks.55 The
addition to interview length from these new questions was partially offset by the removal of the
module about experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic that was added for the 2022 survey and
the removal of some questions regarding families’ financial institutions.56 In some particularly
complicated cases, the amount of time needed was more than three hours. The role of the inter-
viewers in this effort is also critical. Without their dedication and perseverance, the survey would
not be possible.


In 2025, the majority of interviews were completed by telephone, although in-person interviews
were allowed if the respondent preferred that method.57 Each interviewer used a program running
on a tablet to administer the survey and collect the data.

     Weighting
     To provide a measure of the frequency with which families similar to the sample families could be
     expected to be found in the population of all families, an analysis weight is computed for each
     case, accounting for both the systematic properties of the sample design and differential patterns
     of nonresponse. The SCF response rates are lower than some other major government surveys,
     and analysis of the data confirms that the tendency to refuse participation is highly correlated with
     net worth. However, unlike other surveys, which almost certainly also have differential nonre-
     sponse by wealthy households, the SCF has the means to adjust for such nonresponse. Much of
     SCF research is devoted to the evaluation of nonresponse, which allows for continuous improve-
     ment in the analysis weights of the survey that adjust for nonresponse.59


     Sources of Error
     Errors may be introduced into survey results at many stages. Sampling error—the variability
     expected in estimates based on a sample instead of a census—is a particularly important source
     of error. Such error can be reduced either by increasing the size of a sample or, as is done in the
     SCF, by designing the sample to reduce important sources of variability. Sampling error can be
     estimated, and for this report, we use replication methods to do so.


     Replication methods draw samples, called replicates, from the set of actual respondents in a way
     that incorporates the important dimensions of the original sample design. In the SCF, weights

Benchmarking and Comparison with External Data
As part of the general reconciliations required for this report, the survey data were compared with
many external estimates.63 One particularly important comparison is between the SCF and the
Federal Reserve’s Statistical Release Z.1, “Financial Accounts of the United States,” for the
household sector.64 This comparison suggests that when the definitions of the variables in the
two sources are adjusted to a common conceptual basis across multiple asset and debt catego-
ries, the estimates of totals in the two systems tend to be close and show similar growth rates.65


In general, the median values for income and net worth in the SCF are most comparable with
analogous values in other household surveys. However, because of the special dual-frame design
of the SCF sample, different units of observation, and differences in other methodological
aspects, the SCF and other surveys are unlikely to produce identical estimates.
